Part 1 · Password managers - the basics

Plain-language answers that apply to any password manager, not only this one.

The basics

What is a password manager?

A password manager is an app that keeps your logins (usernames, passwords, notes and often one-time codes) in an encrypted store that opens with one master password, a key file or your fingerprint. You remember one strong secret; the app remembers everything else.

It can also invent long random passwords for you and fill them into apps and websites, so you never have to type or reuse them.

Why use one instead of my browser, a notes app, or the same password everywhere?
  • The same password everywhere means one leaked website opens all your accounts. This is the single biggest risk a password manager removes.
  • Notes apps, spreadsheets and photos are usually not encrypted, fill nothing in for you, and are easy to sync or share by accident.
  • Passwords you invent yourself tend to be short and predictable. Generated ones are not.
  • Your browser's built-in manager is much better than reusing passwords and a fine first step. A dedicated manager adds a vault you control and can lock separately, works the same across apps and browsers, and does not depend on one browser or one company account.
What is a data breach, and why does it matter for my passwords?

A breach is when attackers steal data from a company's servers, often including email addresses and passwords. Stolen lists are then tried automatically on other websites, because many people reuse passwords.

If every account has its own random password, a breach at one website costs you one account, not your whole online life. After a breach notice, change the password for that one site.

Native Creds does not check your passwords against breach lists, because it never goes online (see how sync works with no internet permission).

Isn't it risky to keep all my passwords in one place?

It is a trade-off, but a good one. The vault is encrypted: someone would need both the file and your master password (and key file, if you use one). The file on its own is just scrambled data.

Compared with reused or weak passwords, that is far safer. The real risks are a weak master password and losing the only copy of the file, so choose a strong master password and keep a backup (see backup and moving to a new phone).

How often should I change my passwords?

Not on a schedule. Change a password when a website reports a breach, when you think someone saw it, or when you find an old account that still uses a weak or reused one. Strong, unique passwords do not wear out.

Staying safe

How do encryption and a master password protect my passwords?

Your vault is a single file. Your master password is never stored in it. Instead the app runs the password through a deliberately slow calculation to produce an encryption key, and that key scrambles the file with a strong cipher (KeePass files can use AES, ChaCha20 or Twofish). Without the right password the file looks like random noise.

The slow calculation is on purpose: it makes every guess expensive for an attacker. It is also why unlocking can take a few seconds.

What happens if I forget my master password?

It cannot be recovered, by anyone. Not by the developer of Native Creds, who has no copy and no server, and not by any other KeePass app. That is the price of there being no back door.

  • Write the master password down and keep the paper somewhere physically safe, such as a drawer at home.
  • If fingerprint unlock still works, the vault can still be opened. Create a new vault with a new master password and copy your entries across (select entries, then "Copy to another vault"), because Native Creds cannot change an existing vault's master password yet.
  • An older backup of the file still opens with the password it had when it was saved.
How strong should my master password be?

Length beats cleverness. A good master password is a passphrase of five or six random, unrelated words (or something equally long that you can type from memory), used nowhere else and shared with no one.

  • Let dice or a generator pick the words; people choose less randomly than they think.
  • Avoid names, dates, song lyrics, keyboard patterns and anything you have used on a website.
  • A key file adds a second ingredient, something you have as well as something you know.

Native Creds shows a strength meter while you type it when creating a vault.

Cloud-account managers versus local-file managers: what is the difference?

Cloud-account managers (the big subscription services) keep your encrypted data on the company's servers and sync it for you. You get easy sharing, account recovery options and support, but you depend on the company: its servers, its prices, its security and its continued existence.

Local-file managers keep your vault as a file you own. There is no account and no company holding it. You decide where the file lives, and you sync it by placing it somewhere your devices can reach. In exchange you look after backups yourself and there is no "forgot password" button.

Neither is always better. Native Creds is the local-file kind.

What are KeePass and KDBX, and why does it matter that the format is open?

KeePass is a long-established free password manager for computers. Its file format, .kdbx, is publicly documented, and many independent apps now read and write it (KeePassXC, KeePassDX, Keepass2Android, Strongbox, KeePassium and others).

That means your passwords are not locked into one app or one company. If an app disappears, you open the same file in another one. Native Creds reads and writes KDBX 3 and 4. It is an independent app and is not affiliated with the KeePass project.

Codes, passkeys & autofill

What are one-time codes (TOTP) and two-factor authentication (2FA)?

Two-factor authentication asks for a second proof besides your password. The common kind is a six-digit code that changes every 30 seconds, made by an authenticator app.

The code comes from a secret key the website shows you once (as a QR code or as text) plus the current time. The site and your app calculate the same number independently. Anyone who gets the secret key can make the codes, so treat it like a password.

Should I keep my two-factor codes in the same app as my passwords?

It is convenient, but it is a trade-off: whoever opens the vault gets both the password and the code, so the two "factors" live in one place. For most accounts that is still a big improvement over having no second factor.

For your most valuable accounts (your main email, banking) some people prefer a separate authenticator app or a hardware security key. Native Creds works either way; it is your call.

What are passkeys, and does Native Creds support them?

Passkeys are a newer way to sign in without a typed password. Your device or password manager holds a secret key and proves it to the website after you unlock with a fingerprint, face or PIN. Because they only work on the real website, they resist phishing.

Native Creds does not support passkeys yet. It cannot create, store or use them. Passkeys are planned for after launch, with no date promised. Passkeys saved in Google or on your phone keep working exactly as before, and passkeys are not part of the CSV exports used for importing.

What is autofill, and is it safe?

Autofill lets a password manager fill the username and password on a login screen in an app or website, so you do not copy and paste. It is generally safer than typing: the manager matches the app or website address, so a lookalike phishing page gets no suggestion, which is itself a warning sign. Nothing sits on the clipboard either.

The caution: turn autofill on only for a service you trust, because Android shows that service the structure of the sign-in screens you use. Native Creds handles this on your device only, never stores it (apart from an optional log you can switch on), and always makes you unlock and tap the suggestion first.

Why should I let the app generate my passwords?

Because you do not have to remember them. The manager remembers, so each account can have a long random password that no one could guess and that is different everywhere. Random passwords from a proper generator are far harder to crack than anything a person makes up.

Part 2 · Native Creds

How the app works, what it can and cannot do, and what it does with your information.

Getting started

What is Native Creds?

Native Creds is a password manager for Android that keeps your passwords in a KeePass-format vault file that you own. There is no account, no server of ours, no ads and no analytics.

It has folders and search, autofill in apps and browsers, fingerprint or screen-lock unlock, two-factor codes, file attachments, a password generator, CSV import from other managers, and sync through your own cloud drive with automatic merging. It needs Android 8.0 or newer.

Do I need an account? How does it work without a server?

No account, ever. Everything happens inside the app on your phone. Your vault is an encrypted file in a place you pick with Android's file picker. There is no Native Creds server, so there is nobody to sign up with, nobody who holds your data, and also nobody who can reset it for you.

How do I create my first vault, and where should I store the file?
  1. Open Native Creds and tap Create a new vault.
  2. Give it a name, choose a master password (and optionally switch on a key file and fingerprint unlock).
  3. Android's file picker asks where to save the .kdbx file.

Where? Phone storage is private to you but is lost with the phone unless you copy it elsewhere. A cloud storage app such as Google Drive gives you a copy off the phone and lets a second device use the same vault. The file is encrypted either way, but a cloud copy sits on someone else's server, which is one more reason to pick a strong master password.

Should I use a master password, a key file, or both?

A key file is a small random file, generated by the app, that acts like a physical key.

  • Password only is the simplest and fine if the password is strong.
  • Key file only means nothing to memorise, but anyone holding the file can open the vault.
  • Both is the strongest, but losing either one loses the vault.

Store the key file away from the vault file; keeping them in the same synced folder defeats the point. If you turn on fingerprint unlock for a vault that has a key file, Native Creds remembers the key file on that phone, so the phone's lock becomes what protects it there.

Can I have more than one vault? What are "My vaults" and the default vault?

Yes, as many as you like, for example a personal one and a shared family one. My vaults on the home screen lists the ones you opened recently so you can reopen them without the file picker. "Remove from My vaults" only takes it off the list; the file is never deleted.

The default vault is the one the home screen and autofill open directly. Set it from a vault's ⋮ menu ("Make default vault") or in Settings. Without a default, autofill asks which vault to use each time.

You can also select entries and copy them into another vault.

Sync & devices

The app has no internet permission, so how can it sync?

Native Creds itself never goes online. If your vault file is in Google Drive (or another storage app), then that app does the uploading and downloading. Android lets apps read and write such files through its system file picker. Native Creds reads the file, merges any changes, and writes it back; your storage app moves the bytes.

Any storage app that shows up in Android's file picker and lets apps save back to its files can host a vault. Google Drive is the one tested on real phones; others such as Dropbox or OneDrive appear the same way but have not each been verified. If a provider behaves oddly, use Sync now.

How do I set up sync between two phones?
  1. On the first phone, create the vault in Google Drive (or move it there).
  2. On the second phone, install Native Creds, tap Open an existing vault, pick Drive, and choose the same file.
  3. Use both phones normally. Native Creds checks the file when you unlock, when you come back to the app and before you edit, and merges on every save.

Both phones need access to the same Drive file (the same Google account, or the file shared with the other account with edit rights).

What happens if two phones change the vault? How are conflicts handled?

When you save, Native Creds first re-reads the file. If another device changed it, the two versions are combined instead of one overwriting the other:

  • Entries added on either phone are kept.
  • If the same entry was edited on both, the newer edit wins and the older version is kept in that entry's history inside the file.
  • A deletion on one phone carries over, unless the entry was edited later on the other phone.

It is not merged field by field: one whole version of an entry wins. You will see "Merged changes made on another device" when this happens.

What is "Sync now", and when does Native Creds check for changes by itself?

Sync now fetches the newest copy of the file and merges it right away, then tells you what happened. Find it in an open vault's ⋮ menu, by pulling down on the list, or under Settings > Sync details. It can take a few seconds while your storage app fetches the file.

Automatically, Native Creds checks when you unlock, when you return to the app, and before you edit, and it merges on every save.

It says "Changes not saved to the vault file". What should I do?

Native Creds refused to overwrite the file because something looked unsafe: the file was empty or only partly synced, it could not be read right now, another device saved at almost the same moment, another device changed the vault's master password or key file, or the merge could not be verified. Nothing was overwritten, and your changes are still in the app.

  1. Keep the app open and tap Try again after a moment; cloud files are sometimes mid-upload.
  2. If it keeps failing, tap Save a copy… to keep your changes in a new file, then sort things out from there.
  3. Settings > Sync details shows what the app saw on each open, sync and save, without any entry titles or passwords.

Do not lock or close the app first: until saved, those changes exist only in memory.

Autofill & unlocking

How do I turn on autofill?
  1. In Native Creds, open Set as default on the home screen, or Settings > Autofill, and tap Choose Native Creds as autofill service.
  2. In Android's settings choose Native Creds (on Android 14 and newer: Passwords, passkeys & accounts > Preferred service; on older versions: Settings > System > Languages & input > Autofill service).
  3. Open a login page or app, tap the username or password field, tap the "Unlock Native Creds to autofill" bar, unlock, and pick the login.

Set a default vault so autofill opens the right one straight away.

Autofill does not appear in some app or website. What should I check?
  • Settings should say "Native Creds is your autofill service".
  • Use Chrome 135 or newer. Chrome has no separate switch; it follows the Android setting.
  • The entry's URL must contain the website's address.
  • If the suggestion vanished, tap a different field and then the first one again, reload the page, or touch and hold the field and choose Autofill.
  • If your keyboard supports it (Gboard does), the suggestion also appears in its suggestion strip.
  • Some apps and websites block autofill on purpose, and nothing can be done for those.

Still stuck? Open Settings > Autofill setup & diagnostics, switch on the autofill activity log, try again, and read the newest line there.

Will Native Creds offer to save new passwords?

Yes. After you sign in somewhere new, Android shows its "Save" prompt for Native Creds. Accept it and the login is stored in your vault, as a new entry or as an update to the matching one.

How does fingerprint (quick) unlock work, and what are its limits?

It is off until you turn it on, separately for each vault (when creating the vault, or later in its ⋮ menu). Native Creds then stores the vault's master password (and key file) encrypted with a key in the Android Keystore, which Android only releases after your fingerprint or, on Android 11 and newer, your screen lock.

  • It stands in for your master password on that phone, so it is only as strong as your phone's lock. Anyone who can unlock your phone, or whose fingerprint is enrolled on it, can open the vault.
  • On Android 10 and older only strong biometrics (fingerprint) work, not the screen-lock PIN.
  • If you add or remove a fingerprint, Android invalidates the key. Type the master password once and turn quick unlock on again.
  • Native Creds cannot choose which biometric Android offers. On phones that rate face unlock as strong (for example Pixel 8 and newer) it may appear next to fingerprint.
  • The master password always works as a fallback.
When does the vault lock itself?

Immediately when you leave the app: pressing home, switching to another app or turning the screen off. You can also tap the lock icon, or press back at the top level. There is no timer to set. Opening a web link from the app, such as these help pages, also counts as leaving, so the vault locks.

Moving in & out

Can I import from my browser or another password manager?

Yes, from a CSV export: Google Password Manager and Chrome, Edge, Firefox, Bitwarden, Safari / Apple Passwords, LastPass, Dashlane, NordPass and Proton Pass. Import shows per-source export steps, lets you review duplicates before anything is added, and marks imported entries with an "Imported" tag. Google, Chrome, Edge, Firefox and Bitwarden are the most thoroughly checked; the others follow each service's documented export layout.

Two-factor codes come across when the export includes them (Bitwarden, LastPass, Dashlane, Apple and Proton Pass do; Google's does not). Passkeys cannot be imported. 1Password's own format is not supported yet.

A CSV file holds your passwords as plain text. Native Creds offers to delete it afterwards; also remove it from your cloud trash or downloads folder.

Can I use a vault I already have? Is it compatible with KeePassXC, Strongbox, KeePassium and KeePassDX?

Yes. Native Creds opens KDBX 3 and 4 files protected by a password, a key file or both, including the common ciphers (AES, ChaCha20, Twofish). Tap Open an existing vault, or use "Open with" from a file manager, Drive or a mail attachment.

Vaults from KeePassXC, Strongbox and KeePassium were tested in both directions: they open, edit and save in Native Creds and the results open again in those apps, with two-factor codes, attachments, tags and icons intact. KeePassDX, Keepass2Android and KeePass 2 use the same format and the same two-factor fields, but have not each been tested hands-on.

Custom fields added by other apps show in an "Other fields" card that you can view and copy, but not yet edit.

How do I back up my vault or move to a new phone?

Your vault is just a file, so a backup is a copy of it. Copy the .kdbx file with any file manager, keep it in a cloud drive, or use Save a copy… in a vault's ⋮ menu. Keep a copy of the key file too if you use one, in a different place.

On a new phone: install Native Creds, tap Open an existing vault, pick the file (from Drive, a USB transfer or wherever it is) and enter your master password. Native Creds' own settings are deliberately kept out of Android backups, so on the new phone turn autofill and quick unlock on again and choose your default vault.

What if I lose my phone?

The vault is encrypted, so whoever finds the phone still needs your master password (and key file). Lock or wipe the phone with Google's find-your-device tools, install Native Creds on a new phone and open your vault from Drive or your backup.

If the only copy of the vault was on the lost phone, it is gone, which is why a backup matters. If you are worried (a weak master password, or a phone with no screen lock and quick unlock on), change the passwords for your most important accounts, starting with your email.

Using the app

How do I set up two-factor codes in Native Creds?
  1. On the website's two-factor setup page, choose "can't scan the code" or "enter key manually".
  2. In the entry, tap Set up two-factor code and paste the secret key, or an otpauth:// link.
  3. The entry now shows the current code with a countdown.

There is no QR scanner yet. Codes already stored by KeePassXC, KeePassDX or KeePass 2 are recognised automatically. After autofill fills a login that has a code, Native Creds copies the current code so you can paste it on the next page (Settings > Autofill can turn this off). Keep each site's recovery codes somewhere safe too.

Can Native Creds create strong passwords for me?

Yes. When adding or editing an entry, use the generator next to the password field. Choose a length from 8 to 64 characters (18 by default), which character types to include, and optionally leave out look-alike characters such as 0, O, 1, l and I. Passwords are made on your phone with Android's secure random number generator.

How does clipboard auto-clear work?

A copied username, password or two-factor code is marked as sensitive and cleared after 30 seconds, also if you have switched to another app, and at once when the screen turns off. The 30 seconds is fixed for now.

One catch: while Native Creds is behind another app, Android does not let it look at the clipboard, so anything else you copy in those 30 seconds is cleared too. Until it clears, your keyboard or a clipboard-sync feature may be able to read what you copied.

Why can't I take screenshots of the app?

On purpose. Native Creds blocks screenshots, screen recording and the preview in Android's recent-apps list, so a passing glance or a screen-sharing app cannot capture your vault. Screenshots of it come out black.

Can I attach files to an entry?

Yes: recovery codes, a scan, a key file. Attachments are stored encrypted inside the vault. Files over 5 MB trigger a warning and files over 20 MB are refused, because big files make the vault slow to open and sync. Save to… writes an ordinary unencrypted copy, so Native Creds warns you first. There is no in-app preview, and a removed attachment stays in the entry's history, so the vault does not shrink at once.

How do I organise entries, and can I undo a delete?

Make folders from a vault's ⋮ menu, search the whole vault from the search box (results show their folder), and sort from ⋮ > Sort by. Touch and hold an entry to select several, then move, copy to another vault or delete them.

Deleting is permanent. Native Creds has no recycle bin and no undo, so it asks first. (If a vault from another KeePass app has a recycle bin, its entries are hidden from search and autofill.)

Privacy & permissions

What permissions does the app ask for, and why?
  • Biometric / fingerprint: to show Android's own prompt for quick unlock. Android handles the fingerprint; the app never sees it.
  • Foreground service (short): only to keep the 30-second clipboard timer running if you leave the app right after copying.
  • Autofill service: not a permission dialog but a switch you turn on yourself in Android settings.

It does not have the internet permission, and it asks for no storage, contacts, location, camera, microphone or accessibility access. Files are only touched through Android's file picker, and only the ones you choose.

What does Native Creds store on my phone?

In its private app storage only, never in Android's cloud backup: your settings, the list of vaults you opened, an encrypted copy of each cloud vault as last saved on that phone (used to repair sync mix-ups), the optional logs (times, sizes and app names, never passwords or usernames), and, if you turn on quick unlock, the vault's key encrypted behind your fingerprint or screen lock. Uninstalling the app or clearing its storage erases all of that; your vault files stay where you saved them.

The full details are in the privacy policy.

Can the developer, Google or anyone else see my passwords?

The developer cannot. The app has no internet permission, so it cannot send anything anywhere, and it has no analytics or crash reporting. If you keep your vault in Google Drive, Google stores the encrypted file but cannot read it without your master password. Google Play and Android collect their own standard information as part of running the store and the phone; that is separate from the app, which sends nothing.

Is Native Creds free? Are there ads?

There are no ads, no trackers, no subscription and no in-app purchases in this version, and the app is free to install.

Is Native Creds open source?

Not at the moment: the source code is not published, and the app has not had an independent security audit. What keeps you safe from lock-in is the vault format. KDBX is open and documented, so your data can always be opened by other apps. And because the app has no internet permission, Android itself prevents it from sending your data off the phone.

Help & limits

What does Native Creds not do yet?
  • Passkeys (planned for after launch, no date).
  • Scanning a QR code to set up two-factor codes (paste the key instead).
  • Changing a vault's master password inside the app (a KeePass app on a computer can do it, or create a new vault and copy entries across).
  • Editing custom fields created by other apps (they are view and copy only).
  • Importing 1Password's own export format.
  • Previewing attachments, an adjustable clipboard timer or auto-lock timer, and breach checking.
  • Merging field by field: when two phones edit the same entry, one whole version wins and the other is kept in history.
  • Versions for iPhone, Mac or Windows, or a browser extension. Native Creds is Android only for now.
How do I report a bug or send feedback?

Email ManideepsCraftyApps+nativecreds@gmail.com. Say what you did, what you expected, what happened, your Android version and phone model, and the app version.

For sync or autofill problems, the Sync details and autofill logs in Settings are safe to share: they contain times, sizes and app names, never passwords or usernames (the sync log does include the vault's file name). Never send your vault file, your master password or a screenshot showing passwords.
